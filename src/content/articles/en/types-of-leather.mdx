“Full grain”, “top grain”, “genuine leather”, “split”… Shop descriptions are full of words that sound alike and do not mean the same thing. This guide explains where each quality comes from in the hide, which animal and tanning to choose, and how thick your leather should be for your project.

## A hide, layer by layer

A hide is not uniform through its thickness. On top is the **grain**: tight, dense fibres with the natural surface of the skin, its pores and small marks. Below is the **corium**, thicker, with looser fibres. At the bottom, the **flesh side** is the rough side you see on the back of your leather.

Hides are usually too thick to use as they are, so tanneries split them in their thickness. Where the cut is made, and what is done to the surface afterwards, decides the quality of the leather.

## The qualities of leather

### Full grain

The grain is kept intact: not sanded, not corrected. It is the strongest leather, because the tightest fibres are still there, and the most honest one: you see the real surface of the skin, small scars included. It develops a beautiful patina over time.

This is what I use for my pieces, and what I recommend for wallets, cardholders and anything you will carry every day.

### Corrected grain (top grain)

The surface is lightly sanded to remove marks, then usually covered with a pigment layer and sometimes embossed with an artificial grain. It looks more uniform and resists stains better, but it ages less: no real patina, and once the coating wears, it does not recover.

In English, “top grain” is often used for this quality. The name is confusing: it does not mean “the best grain”.

### Nubuck and suede

Both are sanded to raise a soft nap. **Nubuck** is sanded on the grain side, so it keeps much of the strength of the grain. **Suede** is sanded on the flesh side or on a split, so it is softer and weaker. Both stain easily.

### Split

The lower layer, cut from under the grain. It has no grain of its own: it is often sanded into suede, or coated and embossed to imitate a grain leather. It is less strong and should be kept for linings or pieces that do not work much.

### Bonded leather

Leather scraps and fibres glued together into a sheet. Avoid it: it peels and tears quickly, and it is not really leather any more.

## Which animal?

The animal changes the thickness, the softness and the look of the grain.

- **Cow** gives large, strong hides. It is the leather of most belts, bags and wallets, and of most of the vegetable-tanned leathers I use.
- **Vachette** is the French name for leather from young cows: finer and more supple than adult cow.
- **Calf** has a very fine, tight grain and is supple even when thin. Perfect for elegant small goods.
- **Goat** is strong for its thickness, with a pebbled grain. Great for linings, pouches and pieces that must stay thin.

## Which tanning?

Tanning turns a raw hide into a stable material. It changes how the leather behaves on your workbench.

- **Vegetable-tanned** (veg-tan) leather is tanned with plant tannins. It is firm, holds its shape, takes tooling and stamping, burnishes beautifully on the edges and patinas with time. I explain why I use it in [why I use Tuscan vegetable-tanned leather](/guides/tuscan-vegetable-tanned-leather/).
- **Chrome-tanned** leather is softer, more stable in colour and more resistant to water, but its edges do not burnish well: they are usually painted.
- **Combination** tannings, like *latigo*, mix both for a supple yet strong leather.

## Reading a supplier’s label

- **Thickness** is given in millimetres in Europe, and often in ounces (oz) elsewhere: 1 oz is about 0.4 mm. A “3–4 oz” leather is about 1.2 to 1.6 mm thick.
- **Whole hide, side, shoulder, bend**: the part of the hide you buy. The back (bend or *croupon*) is the densest and most regular; the shoulder is a little looser; the belly is the softest and stretchiest.

## Which thickness for which project?

For small goods like cardholders and wallets, **1.2 to 1.4 mm** is ideal. For thicker pieces such as bags, or projects where several layers overlap, go from **1.5 mm without exceeding 2 mm**: beyond that, the piece quickly becomes too thick.
